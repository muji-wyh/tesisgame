# Interaction feel and review

Read this for animation, feedback, sound, reward pacing, or claims that gameplay
feels weak, slow, floaty, noisy, or disconnected. The following are diagnostic
options, not an instruction to apply every effect.

## Find the missing cause

| Observed weakness | Inspect first | Possible targeted improvement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Input feels disconnected | Input acknowledgement, delayed event dispatch | Respond immediately while the larger action develops |
| Object feels like paper | Pivot, rigid parts, occlusion, contact shadow | Articulate the real parts and preserve their volume |
| Motion feels weightless | Acceleration, support, counter-motion, settling | Ground contact and tune momentum with a short controlled settle |
| Buildup feels monotonous | Contrast, pulse spacing, resistance, real progress | Change pulse cadence and articulation with progress, reserving headroom |
| Final release feels weak | Event synchronization, sound attack, peak hierarchy | Align a decisive action, transient, and directional light with visible recoil |
| Final pause feels broken | Frozen clocks, sustained tension, neighboring curves | Maintain tension through a deliberate brief hold or remove an unmotivated gap |
| Screen feels busy | Competing motion, saturation, labels, effect frequency | Remove redundant signals and establish one primary focal event |
| Rewards feel routine | Frequency and variation relative to effort | Reserve elaborate celebration for meaningful outcomes |

## Calibrate the feel target

Use inspected gameplay with comparable action frequency, control scheme, and
intended weight as a timing anchor. A rare cinematic celebration is not a useful
default for a frequent hit. Inspect normal-speed motion and actual playback;
limit claims to the channels that were available.

For a pacing diagnosis, inspect a representative play loop, not just its reward
clip. Extract useful event relationships and contrasts, rather than copying a
reference's exact curves or duration. Preserve this game's specified timing,
mechanics, and intended tone.

## Shape the event

Describe the relevant beats in actual time or normalized progress: contact,
anticipation, commitment, release, settling, and return to play. Not every action
needs every beat. A rapid repeated hit often needs an immediate, short envelope;
a deliberate opening can support anticipation. Keep the requested total duration.

Use a shared event source for motion, sound, and effect emission. Define when an
action becomes committed so visual completion agrees with cancellation and reward
logic. Effects should follow the gameplay state rather than invent an independent
timer that completes after an interrupted action.

For progressive intensity, adjust deliberate variables: pulse interval, motion
amplitude, lock or material response, harmonic density, light leakage, and release
contrast. Select a few appropriate to the material. Preserve perceptible change
near the climax; reaching maximum intensity too early leaves nowhere to go.

Give light a source and geometry: an opening seam, core, strike point, or revealed
interior. Use theme color in the body of the effect and highlight the peak when
appropriate. Inspect how it illuminates or obscures the object. Avoid a detached
full-screen flash as the only evidence of energy.

For tactile motion, reason about joints, mass, contact, and follow-through. A metal
case, elastic toy, drifting crystal, and cloth creature need different curves.
Do not apply the same bounce/scale preset to every material. Keep transform roots
and layout sizing stable so content changes cannot create accidental jumps.

## Make sound belong to the action

Choose appropriate recorded or authored assets for contact, tension, impact, and
tail when the action benefits from those layers. Align the key audible transient
with the visual event, accounting for leading silence and scheduling. Cue start
time alone does not establish perceptual synchronization.

Keep room in the mix for the release. Avoid clipping, an indiscriminate gain boost,
or dozens of overlapping cues. Test the actual game mix, including music, speech,
and frequent actions. In voice-controlled games, treat effect interference with
recognition as a device-test question; do not assert improved recognition from
visual inspection or silently change production volume.

## Compare useful evidence

Use the same event, display size, input sequence, and volume for comparisons.
Check the relevant idle, press, success, miss, cancel, replay, and interrupted
states. For frequent feedback, test a run of actions instead of only the best
isolated moment. Verify reduced motion and muted play still communicate outcome.

Inspect clips at normal speed with audio; use frame stepping only to diagnose
specific alignment issues. Label a source preview, local render, simulated device,
and physical device recording accurately. State unavailable evidence rather than
presenting a proposed effect as a verified sensory improvement.

When prioritizing, use an evidence table only if it helps the task:

| Observation | Player consequence | Proposed change | Validation | Dependency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Body scales with lid | Rigid chest reads flat | Separate hinge and body support | Same-size opening comparison | Layered lid asset |

Estimate asset acquisition/authoring, integration, tuning, and verification
separately when the user requests cost. A shader or tween estimate alone does not
cover missing artwork, animation preparation, sound design, or device testing.
